# Chapter 11 — Overlaying Events onto the Demand Baseline

## Purpose

Earlier chapters of the Demand & Supply Chain app build the **baseline demand plan** through statistical forecasting, collaborative inputs, seasonality, and growth assumptions. Chapter 11 introduces the next layer: **discrete, named events** that overlay on top of that baseline to capture activity the statistical engine cannot see.

Event types the app is designed to accommodate include:

- **Promotion planning** — temporary uplifts driven by trade/marketing activity.
- **Opportunity and risk management** — known wins/losses, tenders, supply risks.
- **Product lifecycle changes** — new product introductions (NPI), phase-outs, and the **cannibalization** they cause on existing SKUs.

This chapter focuses on **NPI** as the worked example because it touches every moving part: the master data hub, valid combinations, the demand subset, the NPI planning module, and the cannibalization sub-model. The end-state is a demand plan that shows the **net impact of product lifecycle activity** on top of the baseline.

Outcome:

- A baseline demand plan from earlier chapters.
- A new (not-yet-in-ERP) product modeled with launch curve, seasonality, annual volume, and price.
- Existing SKUs reduced by a configured cannibalization share.
- All of this visible in the main demand page (350) under a **Product Lifecycle Impact** section.

## Steps covered per sub-video

### 11-02 — Create a Temporary Product (Data Hub)

The problem: a new SKU often has no ERP code yet, but the business already needs to plan against it. The Data Hub provides a **Temporary Products** area (mirrored by Temporary Customers) for this.

Workflow:

1. **Stage the product** in the Data Hub's temporary-products screen — pick its parent in the product hierarchy, give it a display name, flag it as `Temporary`. Submitting generates a temporary code.
2. **Add it to the Forecast Subset** — the product subset that drives demand planning visibility. Without this flag the placeholder won't appear downstream.
3. **Commit the update** — pushes the staged item into the actual product hierarchy.
4. **Mark valid customer-product combinations** — the intersect that controls which customers can plan against which products.
5. **Run `Update ALL Master Hierarchy`** process (page 230DP) — propagates the new product from the Data Hub through to the Demand Planning app.
6. **Confirm in Demand Planning** (page 350) — clear the product filter and verify the new SKU appears in the hierarchy.

Once visible, the product can be planned three ways:

- Direct entry / override of the demand plan,
- Collaborative planning (base level + growth + seasonality), or
- The dedicated **NPI methodology** covered next.

### 11-03 — NPI Planning

Once the temporary product exists, the NPI workflow lets you model its launch explicitly.

1. **Mark the product as NPI** on page **220**: override its planning status to `NPI` and set a **launch date** (e.g. 7 July). Launch status and dates can be customer-specific if needed.
2. **Run the NPI update process** to commit the product as NPI-planned.
3. **Capture NPI assumptions** on page **222**:
   - **Annual volume** at steady-state (BAU).
   - **Seasonality profile** chosen from the library of seasonal curves built in earlier chapters.
   - **Launch curve** — the ramp-up shape from zero up to BAU volume in the weeks following launch (linear ramp used here; richer curves can be configured separately).
   - **Sales price** for the product.
4. The page immediately shows the **modeled weekly launch** beginning in the launch week and extending across the launch horizon.
5. On page **350**, the NPI volume flows through to the demand plan and appears under the **Product Lifecycle Impact** section of the bottom table.

### 11-04 — Cannibalization

A new product rarely creates pure incremental demand — it usually steals share from existing SKUs. The app models this with explicit **cannibalization relationships** between an NPI product and one or more existing products.

1. **Create cannibalization placeholders** — empty list items used as containers for cannibalization relationships. Because the NPI product is brand-new, its cannibalization "children" must be generated by running the placeholder-creation action.
2. Back on page **222**, with the NPI product selected, scroll to the cannibalization section and **pick an existing product** that will be cannibalized. Selecting the row syncs the detail table below.
3. **Choose a cannibalization strategy** — two equivalent framings:
   - "% of **NPI volume** that is taken from this existing product" (used in the example: 25% of NPI volume is at the expense of the chosen SKU), or
   - "% of **existing product volume** that the NPI will absorb."
4. Repeat per cannibalized SKU.
5. On page **350**, the cannibalized products show the negative impact in their lifecycle section and in the main demand table.

### 11-05 — Temporary Product Replacement (ERP Code Hand-off)

Eventually the new product gets a **real ERP code**. Without intervention, the ERP feed would create the official product as a *separate* item, leaving the NPI plan stranded on the obsolete temporary code.

The Data Hub provides actions to **reassign the official ERP code onto the temporary product** and **retire the temporary code**, so the plan history follows the SKU. This is a **process-design decision for the implementation project**: who triggers the reassignment, on what cadence, and how to reconcile any volume that already loaded under the new ERP code.

## Key takeaways

- **Events sit on top of the baseline**, not inside it — keep the baseline pure (statistical + collaborative) and layer named activity (NPI, promo, opportunity) as additive/subtractive overlays. This preserves auditability and lets each layer be reviewed independently.
- **Temporary products are a Data Hub concern**, not a Demand Planning one. The hierarchy update process and forecast-subset flag are mandatory hand-off steps; without them the placeholder is invisible downstream.
- **Valid combinations matter**: a temporary product must be marked as plannable against the relevant customers, otherwise the intersect blocks input.
- **NPI assumptions are decomposed**: annual volume, seasonality curve, launch (ramp-up) curve, and price are independent inputs — each can be sourced from a different stakeholder and reused across launches.
- **Launch curves and seasonality curves are libraries** — built once (earlier chapters) and reused, which keeps NPI modeling fast and consistent across products.
- **Cannibalization has two equivalent input framings** — "% of NPI volume" vs "% of existing volume." Pick one convention per launch to avoid double-counting.
- **Placeholder lists for cannibalization children** are a common Anaplan pattern: pre-create empty list items and bind them on demand to avoid runtime list creation in user workflows.
- **The ERP-code hand-off is a process gap, not a model gap**: plan early in implementation for how temporary codes inherit the official ERP code so that plan history follows the real SKU. Without a defined process, the model ends up with duplicate (temp + ERP) products and the plan stays on the wrong one.
- **Everything converges on page 350** — the main demand page is where baseline + NPI + cannibalization (and any other event types) are netted together. The **Product Lifecycle Impact** section is the audit trail for the event layer.
